I've been practicing a new habit lately. And it's not some sort of crazy sport, or artsy craft, or ambitious intellectual pursuit. In fact, it's nothing at all.

Stillness, and the disconcerting boredom that often accompanies it, I've realized, is something we often only experience, or perhaps are "graced" with, on sporadic, intermittent occasion.

The placid, bracing nighttime air as you drag your trash cans down to the curb, bouncing and reverberating with a thunderous THUD, PFFF, THUD, as you descend your driveway, having put off this weeknight chore until it pulled you from your peaceful rest on the couch, the remembrance of the task going off like an alarm in your head, and as if the stinky, heavy refuse you have in tow has decided to play a symphony of percussion to the empty neighborhood streets that are washed out by flickering incandescent yellow lights. The mechanical droning of an airplane cabin many hours into a flight, as you uncomfortably re-adjust, struggle, and squirm in your seat in 32B, feeling a tensile pulse through your thighs and calves while you sip on a watered-down, yet surprisingly refreshing, ginger ale, ever so careful not to spill it on the old woman sleeping next to you in 32C, herself having fallen asleep with the movie "50 First Dates" playing on the dim, slightly-scratched screen on the seat back in front of her. The ethereal breeze of frigid October air hitting your face as you unzip the door of your tent on an early fall morning of camping, letting the brisk mountain wind, carrying pine and lichen and soil, flood over your already-cold, yet somehow pungent and sweaty corpus, having been squeezed and soaked by layer upon layer of wool, neoprene, and cloth that grip your joints and limbs, aching with dull pressure, having contended with the frigid ground all night, albeit for the hand-me-down torn and deflated sleeping pad that made a futile attempt to hoist you from the frozen earth below.

These moments of stillness arrive to us without plan or warning. The five eternal minutes as you pace through looming and imposing walls of faux fur coats and cheap accessories in a Nordstrom Rack, waiting for your girlfriend to try clothes on in the dressing room, in what has seemingly turned into an Odyssean task. The awkward shuffle amidst a crowded entryway in a restaurant as you wait for your table, even your very own breathing drowned out by the din of clinking glasses and conversation and happy birthdays and first dates. The mechanical whir of your fan as you lay still in bed at night, drifting in and out of consciousness to its rhythmic hum.

Even though these moments are dull, fleeting, or perhaps even annoying at times, I've come to realize that they are one of the greatest gifts our days incur. Voiding yourself of action, removing intention — save for the sake of intentional stillness — and delivering your mind wholly to ephemeral, unstructured thought: these are some of the rarest opportunities we're afforded, delivered sparingly and seldomly. Instead of welcoming these moments as they come to us as rare gifts, they often become a burden: a heaviness and discomfort, an itch that crawls under your skin and immerses itself under every nerve ending.

So, we numb it. Our phones, distractions, food, drugs — anything to paradoxically quiet the noise of our quiet, uninterrupted internal state. We shun the presence of nothing out of some sort of unfounded, primal disdain for its inevitable existence.

That's why doing nothing has become my most powerful habit as of late. Turning all my screens off after a long day at work and seeing where my mind takes me. Sometimes it's chasing my dogs around the backyard in a chaotic convergence of panting and barking and jumping. Or it's pulling out my cheap, crusted, sticky oil paints and brushes, and attempting to paint a picture I took of the sunset while I listen to old Freddie Gibbs albums on a little Bluetooth speaker, more focused on the feeling of bristle against canvas than the resulting media. It's going for a run without headphones — instead choosing to listen to the cacophony of humming cars from a distance, mixed with the loud chirps of summertime crickets vibrating the grass around my feet, as the humid southern California air overpowers my senses with the smells of blooming eucalyptus and salty marine mist.

This probably comes off as, but truthfully is not, some hippie-dippy, self-help pedestal that I'm trying to preach from. Rather, it is the intentional action of stasis: the uncomfortable, yet ever-revealing presence and acknowledgement of stillness, and the invaluable gift of nothing.

> Here's to the best habit I could engender upon myself — being no habit at all.

_— J_
